## How to use these case studies

Use these as ecommerce trust and UGC system references, not visual styling references. The reusable pattern is to reduce shopper decision effort while preserving source access, authenticity, transparency, accessibility, legal alignment, and scalable design-system behavior.

Inspect visuals that show:

- AI item snapshots, review summaries, aspect summaries, and links back to full details.
- User-testing sections about AI trust, authenticity, accuracy, ethics, and labeling.
- Reviewer dashboards that show impact, pending/posted reviews, ready-to-review items, and contribution stats.
- Gamification explorations, especially points versus badges and status alternatives.
- Review template flows comparing wizard, accordion, hybrid, optional fields, previews, and progress.
- Category-specific review fields that collect useful detail without overwhelming the reviewer.

Skip portfolio navigation and brand framing unless the task is about case-study presentation.

## Case 1: GenAI item discovery summaries

Source: https://www.nicolearoberts.com/case-studies/empowering-item-discovery-with-gen-ai-summaries

### Core lesson

AI summaries should reduce cognitive load without replacing shopper judgment. Walmart's GenAI summaries focused on concise item overviews, review summaries, aspect sentiment, and source access so shoppers could evaluate products faster while still verifying details.

### Design moves to reuse

- Use AI where shoppers face dense item details, scattered review themes, and decision fatigue.
- Separate summary types by job: item snapshot, technical details, reviews summary, aspect summary, and filtered review drill-down.
- Label AI-generated content clearly and explain what corpus it summarizes.
- Link summaries to full item details, specifications, ratings, and original reviews so users can verify.
- Treat trust reactions as part of the design surface: authenticity, accuracy, ethics, privacy, and employee concerns may all shape adoption.
- Align AI modules with the design system while giving them a recognizable identity.
- Build with accessibility, legal, analytics, data science, data operations, and engineering from the start when AI content scales across commerce.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Four-phone overview showing item snapshot, review summary, and aspect overview.
- Competitive references for AI and review summarization.
- User-testing reactions to "Generated by AI."
- Color strategy and consistency explorations.
- Final MVP flow showing AI summaries plus non-AI detail links.

### Red flags to catch

- AI summaries look like original customer reviews or brand copy.
- Users cannot tell whether a summary covers one review, all reviews, or product specs.
- The summary improves speed but removes the verification path that creates confidence.
- The AI module is visually novel but inconsistent with the rest of the commerce system.

## Case 2: Reviewer profile

Source: https://www.nicolearoberts.com/case-studies/creating-a-reviewer-profile-to-drive-engagement-and-impact

### Core lesson

People contribute more when they can see their work, status, and impact. The reviewer profile centralized review writing, tracking, pending/posted status, ready-to-review items, and impact metrics so reviewers felt acknowledged rather than sending feedback into a void.

### Design moves to reuse

- Centralize review management instead of scattering entry points behind past orders, product pages, emails, and prompts.
- Show users what happened to their reviews: pending, posted, helpful votes, views, and influence.
- Use contribution dashboards to make social proof reciprocal; reviews help shoppers, and reviewers should see that help.
- Test motivation mechanics instead of assuming gamification works. Points, badges, status, and impact modules create different mental models.
- Prefer simple, intuitive progression when users find badge systems noisy or unclear.
- Connect reviewer profile work with adjacent programs, such as sampling or incentivized review programs, so users see one coherent experience.
- Launch the core centralized hub when time is tight, then add richer impact or gamification modules after learning from real usage.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Reviewer profile dashboard with impact stats.
- Ready-to-review carousels and dedicated review pages.
- All reviews page with pending and posted tabs.
- Points versus badges testing concepts.
- Status alternatives after conflicting project constraints.
- Push/email impact communication concepts.

### Red flags to catch

- Reviewers do not know whether their feedback was approved, seen, or useful.
- Review entry points exist but are buried in places users do not naturally revisit.
- Gamification adds badges or levels without explaining benefits or impact.
- Multiple contribution programs compete in the same account area with different rules.

## Case 3: Scalable review template

Source: https://www.nicolearoberts.com/case-studies/optimizing-user-feedback-with-a-scalable-review-template

### Core lesson

Review forms should collect richer, item-specific feedback without increasing perceived burden. Walmart's review template evolved from a generic form into a scalable hybrid pattern that balanced required basics, optional detail, category-specific questions, previews, progress, and flexible completion.

### Design moves to reuse

- Define which review inputs are truly critical before adding fields.
- Balance business data needs with user effort, comfort, and willingness to complete.
- Make sensitive or personal fields optional and explain their value when needed.
- Group related inputs to reduce cognitive load and capture high-value information early.
- Compare structural patterns: single page, accordion, wizard, and hybrid flows.
- Use progress, previews, autosave, quick edits, and a positive first step to reduce uncertainty.
- Support category-specific customization inside a consistent cross-vertical template.
- Let users contribute at different levels of detail instead of forcing every reviewer through the longest path.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Competitive analysis of optional fields and wizard flows.
- Review taxonomy and input prioritization.
- Straight versus stepped user-flow concepts.
- Accordion and wizard prototypes.
- User-testing comparison across two rounds with randomized order.
- Final hybrid template with progress, preview, optional fields, autosave, and category-specific details.

### Red flags to catch

- A generic review template asks the same questions for every category even when shoppers need different evidence.
- Required fields create drop-off before the most important feedback is captured.
- Detailed data collection ignores user comfort, especially around personal attributes.
- A form optimizes business insight at the expense of reviewer agency and completion.

## Combined heuristics

### AI summaries

Design AI as a compression layer, not a replacement for evidence. Summaries should help users decide what to inspect next and provide a clear route to source material.

### Review contribution

Design reviewing as an ongoing relationship. Give users a home for reviewable items, submitted reviews, moderation status, contribution impact, and future prompts.

### Motivation

Use recognition carefully. Impact, stats, status, points, badges, and rewards should make contribution feel meaningful, not manipulative or confusing.

### Review forms

Collect the most valuable feedback first, make additional detail optional, and adapt questions to the product category. Rich data is only useful if users finish the flow.

### Scale

For large commerce systems, design the pattern as infrastructure: cross-category enough to reuse, flexible enough for vertical-specific detail, and aligned with accessibility, legal, analytics, engineering, and design-system constraints.
