## How to use these case studies

Use these as product-posture and interaction-system references. The case studies are concise, so reuse their repeated pattern rather than treating them as deep research reports: define the product challenge, reduce friction without removing nuance, map core journeys, clarify priorities, and build a system of screens, states, and reusable patterns.

Inspect visuals that show:

- Mobile-first hero screens and app previews.
- Communications, call, voicemail, and customer-context surfaces.
- Wellbeing check-in and emotional-state screens.
- Payment method, payment journey, and transaction-management screens.
- Life-admin dashboards, document search, trusted contacts, reminders, and legacy-access surfaces.

## Case 1: Ahoy app

Source: https://jacobdilley.com/work/ahoy-app

### Core lesson

Business communication tools should make complex coordination feel lightweight. Ahoy brought numbers, conversations, call handling, and customer context into one place, so the design needed clear hierarchy and predictable next steps rather than feature density.

### Design moves to reuse

- Treat communication as a context-management problem: calls, voicemail, contacts, messages, and customer history should connect instead of living as separate utilities.
- Give users a calm default view that makes the next communication action obvious.
- Use generous spacing and strong grouping so call controls and customer context do not compete.
- Build reusable patterns for common communication states such as missed, active, voicemail, follow-up, and customer handoff.

### Red flags to catch

- A communications app exposes every channel but does not help users decide what needs attention now.
- Call and customer context are separated so users must remember what happened.
- Visual polish hides weak feedback for call state, message state, or follow-up state.

## Case 2: Shrub app

Source: https://jacobdilley.com/work/shrub-app

### Core lesson

Youth wellbeing products need emotional accessibility and structure at the same time. Shrub's opportunity was to make mental health support approachable for young people without oversimplifying the emotional nuance of the experience.

### Design moves to reuse

- Use plain, supportive copy that helps users identify how they feel without making the product sound clinical or childish.
- Keep the first action simple and low-pressure, especially for check-ins or support entry points.
- Use calming visual hierarchy, predictable feedback, and clear pathways to the next supportive action.
- Avoid making emotional exploration feel like a performance, score, or test unless the user understands the purpose.

### Red flags to catch

- The product asks young users for sensitive emotional input before earning trust.
- Wellbeing screens look friendly but lack clear guidance, safety, or support paths.
- The UI flattens emotion into simplistic choices that do not support follow-up action.

## Case 3: Ensembl Payments

Source: https://jacobdilley.com/work/ensembl-payments

### Core lesson

Sensitive payments require visibility, flexibility, and reassurance. Ensembl Payments focused on funeral directors and estate planners, where transactions are operationally complex and emotionally charged.

### Design moves to reuse

- Make payment status, method, amount, due timing, and next action visible without hunting.
- Support flexible payment journeys while keeping each step legible and auditable.
- Reduce manual administration by designing clear collection, tracking, reminder, and reconciliation surfaces.
- Use neutral, calm UI language for funeral, estate, or bereavement-adjacent transactions.
- Make errors and failed payments recoverable with explicit guidance and safe retry paths.

### Red flags to catch

- A payment flow optimizes for speed while ignoring emotional sensitivity or role-based accountability.
- Teams cannot see which payments need action, who owns the next step, or what happened previously.
- Sensitive transactions use overly promotional, playful, or casual copy.

## Case 4: SafeKeep mobile app

Source: https://jacobdilley.com/work/safekeep-mobile-app

### Core lesson

Life-admin products are trust products. SafeKeep asked users to store documents, policies, personal messages, trusted contacts, reminders, and legacy instructions, so the product had to feel secure, calm, structured, and easy to update.

### Design moves to reuse

- Lead with trust: explain what is stored, who can access it, when access happens, and how users stay in control.
- Structure sensitive information into understandable categories such as insurance, home, documents, contacts, reminders, and legacy access.
- Make search and update flows prominent because stored information loses value when it becomes stale.
- Treat trusted contacts and future access as permissioned workflows with clear states, invitations, confirmations, and revocation.
- Use onboarding to build confidence gradually, not to demand every sensitive detail upfront.
- Connect product UX with support, email journeys, and feedback loops; SafeKeep reported improved onboarding completion, fewer support requests, and higher engagement after product and growth improvements.

### Red flags to catch

- The app asks for important life information before users understand security, access, and control.
- Document storage is easy, but retrieval, reminders, updates, and trusted sharing are unclear.
- Legacy or family workflows are buried as settings instead of treated as core trust moments.

## Combined heuristics

### Launch-ready product direction

Turn broad early requirements into a practical system: core journeys, feature priorities, flows, states, reusable patterns, and a visual language that engineering can build from.

### Calm hierarchy

In sensitive products, calm does not mean empty. It means the primary next step is obvious, secondary context is available, and the user never has to guess what state the system is in.

### Trust before depth

Ask for sensitive data gradually. Show why the information matters, how it is protected, who can see it, and how the user can change or revoke it.

### Nuance without friction

Do not flatten complex domains into a single generic flow. Preserve the domain's necessary details while using progressive disclosure, clear grouping, and reusable patterns to keep the experience manageable.

### Measure support load

For sensitive onboarding and operational tools, track onboarding completion, support requests, engagement, failed tasks, and confusion points. A cleaner UI should reduce both user anxiety and team support burden.
