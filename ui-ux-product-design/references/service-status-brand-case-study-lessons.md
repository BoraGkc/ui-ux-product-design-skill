## How to use these case studies

Use these as product reasoning references, not as visual themes to copy. The strongest lessons are about turning vague improvement requests into clearer product jobs: remember what you read, guide high-stakes controllers, check a flight without anxiety, trust a coach marketplace, understand a museum's identity, or grasp what an AI startup actually does.

Inspect visuals that show:

- The before/after or audit view where existing hierarchy creates confusion.
- Mobile status pages, result cards, timelines, and trip detail treatments.
- Research artifacts such as flow charts, documentation synthesis, audits, and user-testing findings.
- Landing-page hero sections where brand expression is paired with concrete product explanation.
- Marketplace pages where trust, expertise, outcomes, and next steps need to be visible quickly.

Skip portfolio cover typography unless the task is specifically about portfolio storytelling or visual identity.

## Case 1: Restore

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/newrestore#casestudy-intro

### Core lesson

AI products work best when features reinforce a behavior loop. Restore is not just an AI summarizer; its summaries, flashcards, highlight connections, and daily reviews all serve one job: helping readers retain and understand non-fiction after highlighting.

### Design moves to reuse

- Start with the user's emotional arc, including the moment when initial enthusiasm turns into frustration.
- Compare existing tools by the underlying job they fail to complete, not only by feature gaps.
- Turn passive content storage into active recall, review, connection-making, and comprehension.
- Make AI output actionable inside the user's routine rather than a detached novelty feature.
- Validate demand before overbuilding: prototype, test interest, ask willingness-to-pay, and use early metrics to shape the MVP.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Personal narrative sections that frame the problem before the product.
- Sketches, wireframes, and prototype progression.
- Mobile screens for highlight study, flashcards, and review.
- Market-validation metrics and waitlist call-to-action.

### Red flags to catch

- The product collects notes, highlights, or saved items but does not help users revisit or apply them.
- AI features summarize content without creating a loop of practice, retrieval, or decision-making.
- A learning app optimizes for quantity captured instead of comprehension gained.

## Case 2: Project MOA

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/new-moa

### Core lesson

High-stakes systems need decision support, not just access to tools. Project MOA reframed air traffic management from a pile of disconnected windows into a guided workspace with prioritized notifications, task direction, a shared map, and just-in-time recommendations.

### Design moves to reuse

- Do the hard domain reading before interviewing experts so questions become more precise.
- Convert dense documentation into flow charts that show actions, artifacts, dependencies, and handoffs.
- Use personas only after research has narrowed the real jobs to be done.
- Replace fragmented tools with spaces, panels, maps, and task lists organized around role and situation.
- Put decision support next to the moment of action, especially when time and safety matter.
- Prioritize onboarding, cognitive load, communication, and burnout as legitimate product outcomes.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Flow chart and documentation synthesis artifacts.
- Persona/jobs-to-be-done section for the air traffic controller.
- Redesigned map and demand/capacity views.
- Central home page, notifications, and task list concepts.
- Before/after examples of disconnected windows versus organized spaces.

### Red flags to catch

- Users have every tool available but still have to hunt for what matters.
- Critical guidance lives in PDFs, policy documents, or other systems outside the workflow.
- Notification design tells users that something happened without clarifying priority or next action.
- A high-stakes workspace modernizes styling while preserving cognitive overload.

## Case 3: Breeze Flight Status

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/breezeflightstatus

### Core lesson

Status pages should reduce anxiety immediately. Breeze's flight-status redesign shows that search forms, result cards, mobile layouts, timelines, and trip detail pages must answer the traveler's core question quickly: what is happening, does it affect me, and what can I do next?

### Design moves to reuse

- Audit the live flow first and write down where users hesitate, scroll unnecessarily, or lose orientation.
- Benchmark competitors for hierarchy, spacing, readability, timeline clarity, and mobile behavior, then synthesize what works.
- Promote an overgrown modal into a full page when the content has become a destination.
- Distinguish result types clearly, especially when direct flights, departing flights, and related trips could look similar.
- Use cards, color, spacing, and labels to support scanning, but back color with readable text.
- Test prototypes with real users and iterate on observed confusion rather than internal preference.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Annotated audit of the original mobile form.
- Airline competitor comparisons.
- Flight status search, result card, and status-color treatments.
- Trip details page replacing a cramped modal.
- User-testing feedback about clarity, simplicity, and card readability.

### Red flags to catch

- The page asks users to complete a form before showing whether results loaded or what changed.
- Status colors are present but labels, hierarchy, or contrast remain weak.
- Mobile users have to scroll below the fold to see the result of their own search.
- Cards are visually blended into the background, making separate options hard to distinguish.

## Case 4: Leland

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/leland

### Core lesson

Education and coaching marketplaces sell trust before they sell scheduling. Leland's design work points to a marketplace job where visitors need to understand who the coaches are, what outcomes they help with, and why the platform is credible enough for high-stakes education and career decisions.

### Design moves to reuse

- Make the marketplace category and user outcome visible before browsing mechanics.
- Pair coach expertise with human proof: role, category, photo, credibility markers, and concrete success language.
- Support both sides of the marketplace when relevant: learners seeking help and experts monetizing expertise.
- Keep expansion across categories from making navigation vague; users should still know which path matches their goal.
- Treat visual polish as a trust signal only when the copy, hierarchy, and calls to action are equally clear.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Coach-facing landing page and call-to-action.
- Coach cards or profile previews.
- Category navigation and browse entry points.
- Hero copy pairing expertise with user outcomes.

### Red flags to catch

- Users are asked to choose a coach before they understand category fit, proof, or expected outcome.
- The page talks about a platform generally but hides the specific transformation users are buying.
- Marketplace growth creates broad navigation that no longer helps users pick a path.

## Case 5: Springville Museum of Art

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/springville-moa

### Core lesson

Cultural institution websites should make identity and visitor intent meet. The Springville Museum of Art redesign frames visual aesthetic and flow as one problem: the site should feel worthy of the institution while helping visitors find art, programs, visits, and giving paths.

### Design moves to reuse

- Let the institution's history, building, collection, and local role inform the visual language.
- Pair expressive typography or art direction with simple navigation labels such as visit, art, programs, and give.
- Give visitors a first-screen sense of place, not only an abstract brand treatment.
- Keep art and institutional content legible; mood should not bury practical visit planning.
- Use redesign work to align perceived quality with the organization's real-world credibility.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Museum homepage hero and navigation.
- Use of building imagery, artwork, and editorial typography.
- Page flow from identity into practical visitor routes.

### Red flags to catch

- A nonprofit or cultural site looks visually generic despite having a distinctive story.
- The homepage expresses personality but hides visit planning, programs, collections, or donation paths.
- Art direction overwhelms legibility, navigation, or accessibility.

## Case 6: Interval

Source: https://chaachiedesigns.framer.website/interval

### Core lesson

AI/SaaS landing pages must translate automation into concrete business outcomes. Interval's redesign uses visual identity to support a clearer explanation of automated accounts-receivable collections and customer communications.

### Design moves to reuse

- State the customer problem in plain language before describing the technology.
- Show the product suite through use cases, workflows, and outcomes rather than abstract AI claims.
- Make "book a demo" or "see how it works" available after the visitor understands the offer.
- Use polished brand expression to increase credibility, but do not let it replace product explanation.
- Connect automation to business value: recovered balances, fewer manual reminders, communication tracking, and healthier operations.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Landing-page hero and primary calls to action.
- Product navigation and suite framing.
- Sections that explain automated collections and communication workflows.

### Red flags to catch

- The page says "AI" repeatedly without showing what task is automated.
- Visitors have to infer who the product is for or what business result it improves.
- Demo calls to action appear before the product value is concrete.

## Combined heuristics

### Consumer learning tools

Design for retained value, not saved content. If users capture knowledge, give them a retrieval loop, practice surface, connection model, and visible evidence that the product improves understanding over time.

### High-stakes operations

Design for guided attention. Consolidate fragmented tools, prioritize tasks, show current state, and place recommendations beside the action they support.

### Travel and logistics status

Design for relief. Lead with status, timing, affected route/item/person, confidence, next step, and notification options. Make mobile layouts work with stressed, distracted, one-handed use.

### Marketplaces

Design for qualified trust. Before conversion, show category fit, expert credibility, outcome evidence, pricing or commitment expectations when relevant, and a reversible path to engage.

### Cultural and nonprofit sites

Design for identity plus action. The site should feel specific to the institution while making practical visitor, program, collection, and support paths obvious.

### SaaS and AI landing pages

Design for comprehension before persuasion. Explain the customer's painful workflow, show how the product changes it, prove credibility, and then ask for the demo, signup, or sales action.
