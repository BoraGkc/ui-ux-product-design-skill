## How to use these case studies

Use these as research-method and product-impact references, not visual style references. The common pattern is:

1. Quantitative data identifies the leak or business risk.
2. Research explains the human cause.
3. The design response targets confidence, hierarchy, timing, or prioritization.
4. Impact is measured honestly, with limits and next tests stated clearly.

Inspect visuals that show:

- Funnel charts, order/return data, heatmaps, or analytics diagnostics.
- Research artifacts from interviews, benchmarking, remote tests, field visits, shadowing, or support walkthroughs.
- Low-fidelity sketches and wireframes that show how evidence shaped structure before polish.
- Before/after navigation, onboarding, status, or prioritization patterns.
- Result sections that separate immediate engagement shifts from slower behavioral or operational change.

Skip hero typography and portfolio navigation unless the task is specifically about case-study storytelling.

## Case 1: Next ecommerce returns reduction

Source: https://www.joependlebury.com/work/next-ecommerce-ux-returns-reduction/

### Core lesson

Returns are often a confidence problem before they are a logistics problem. In the Next suits case, the business saw costly returns, but mixed research showed that shoppers lacked enough sizing, fit, and navigation reassurance to buy decisively.

### Design moves to reuse

- Treat returns, duplicate-size orders, and category drop-off as signals of uncertainty.
- Combine ecommerce analytics with interviews, competitor benchmarking, remote usability testing, and heatmap analysis.
- Benchmark around the user's confidence needs, not around surface feature copying.
- Put destination categories where users naturally scan, especially when shoppers arrive with intent.
- Make fit guidance, measurement help, model/body context, and size confidence central to the buying journey.
- Prioritize low-effort, high-confidence fixes first, then keep the broader roadmap visible.
- Separate fast engagement results from slower returns behavior when reporting impact.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Orders and returns analytics.
- Competitor fit-guide and shop-by-fit comparisons.
- In-person interview setup.
- Remote usability recordings and heatmap/F-pattern evidence.
- Evidence-led concept sketches for the suits journey.
- Roadmap of multiple concepts beyond the first navigation fix.

### Red flags to catch

- The team treats high returns as a policy, warehouse, or customer-behavior issue before checking confidence and product information.
- Fit/size support exists but is buried outside the user's natural scanning path.
- A category page assumes browsing behavior when users arrive with a specific purchase mission.
- The success story overclaims causality when the result was not isolated through controlled testing.

## Case 2: By Miles onboarding activation

Source: https://www.joependlebury.com/work/by-miles-onboarding-ux-research/

### Core lesson

Onboarding abandonment can be a service confidence problem, not just an app-screen problem. By Miles needed customers to activate a physical car tracker, so the critical UX spanned purchase, email, packaging, in-car installation, support, and app feedback.

### Design moves to reuse

- Map the complete post-purchase journey before redesigning the screen with the worst drop-off.
- Experience the setup yourself when the product includes hardware, location, environment, or physical effort.
- Use support contacts and secret-shopper style checks to find terminology, handoff, and availability gaps.
- Break a broad confidence issue into testable problem statements before ideating.
- Use rough sacrificial concepts to learn quickly without making the team defend polished ideas.
- Design progress states around transparency: current state, what is being checked, expected timing, success, failure, and troubleshooting.
- Give users self-serve guidance first and human support as a clear fallback.
- Treat onboarding as a measurable system that continues after one release.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Miles Tracker device and packaging.
- Activation funnel showing the high-abandonment screen.
- Firsthand installation photos in a real car.
- AI-assisted sacrificial concepts for video, support, and AR directions.
- Participant quote about installation difficulty.
- Connecting, success, and troubleshooting wireframes.
- Final activation screens with progress and reassurance.

### Red flags to catch

- The screen says "hold tight" or "loading" without telling users what is happening.
- The product depends on a physical setup but the UX is designed from a desk.
- Packaging, email, app copy, and support teams use inconsistent language.
- Out-of-hours or failed-state support is treated as an edge case even though it shapes trust.
- The team measures screen completion but not support burden, cancellation, or longer confidence.

## Case 3: STERIS healthcare field research

Source: https://www.joependlebury.com/work/healthcare-ux-research-steris/

### Core lesson

Clinical workflow design must reveal urgency, risk, and priority at a glance. In the STERIS case, technicians had information across fragmented systems, but not a decision hierarchy that showed what was urgent, delayed, or at risk.

### Design moves to reuse

- Enter the clinical or operational environment and observe without disrupting the work.
- Adapt research methods to safety constraints; sometimes careful observation matters more than interruptive interviews.
- Ask what the user needs to know at a specific moment to do the job safely.
- Distinguish systems built for data capture from systems built for time-pressured decisions.
- Look for structural analogues in other domains, such as kitchen display systems for queue, status, timer, and urgency management.
- Explore multiple low-fidelity structures before committing: now/next/soon columns, live tiles, swimlanes, filters, and priority locks.
- Root design principles in field evidence before moving to high fidelity or implementation.
- Validate discovery directions with the same users and environments before treating them as solved.

### Visuals worth inspecting

- Endoscope reprocessing environment photos.
- Technician empathy brainstorm.
- McDonald's kitchen display system reference.
- GTD/Trello prioritization reference.
- FigJam Crazy 8s and dot voting.
- First and second wireframe iterations with annotations.
- Tile-based Endoscopy Hub concept.

### Red flags to catch

- The interface records everything but fails to answer "what needs attention now?"
- Safety-critical priority is hidden behind filters, tables, or manual workarounds.
- Research interrupts high-risk work instead of adapting to the environment.
- Design direction is accepted before being tested under realistic operational load.
- High-fidelity UI begins before system dependencies and data inputs are understood.

## Combined heuristics

### Business metrics

Use metrics as smoke, not diagnosis. High returns, abandonment, cancellations, and support contacts identify where to look; they do not prove why users fail.

### Confidence gaps

Design for confidence when users face cost, commitment, uncertainty, or physical setup. Show evidence, steps, timing, fit, status, fallback, and reassurance at the moment doubt appears.

### Mixed-methods research

Combine methods that see different things: analytics for scale, interviews for meaning, usability testing for behavior, heatmaps for attention, benchmarking for alternatives, support audits for service gaps, and field observation for context.

### Small changes

A small UI change is only small after the research is done. If evidence points to a low-effort fix, ship it quickly, but keep the broader system problem and measurement limits visible.

### Field and healthcare work

Design around the cost of interruption. In clinical, industrial, or operational settings, prioritize glanceability, urgency, error prevention, and realistic environmental constraints before visual polish.

### Impact reporting

Report impact with time horizons. Engagement can move in days; returns, cancellations, support demand, and safety outcomes may take longer and require stronger controls to attribute confidently.
